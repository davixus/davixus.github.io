---
title: Why is the sky blue?
description: Sunlight and the atmosphere team up to paint the daytime sky blue.
---

Sunlight looks white, but it contains a rainbow of colors. Each color has a different wavelength: blue light has shorter waves than red light. As sunlight passes through Earth’s atmosphere, it bumps into tiny gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules scatter shorter wavelengths much more strongly than longer ones. This effect is called **Rayleigh scattering**.

The scattered blue light travels across the sky in every direction, so when you look away from the Sun, blue light reaches your eyes from all around you. Violet light has an even shorter wavelength and scatters too, but the Sun produces less violet, some is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are less sensitive to it. The mixture therefore looks blue rather than violet.

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight takes a longer path through the atmosphere to reach you. Much of the blue light gets scattered out of that direct path, leaving more reds and oranges to reach your eyes. Dust, clouds, and pollution can change the exact colors, but the basic reason stays the same: the air scatters blue sunlight toward you.

Clouds look white for a related reason. Their water droplets are much larger than air molecules, so they scatter all visible colors more evenly. Those colors mix together and appear white; a thick cloud can look gray because less sunlight passes through it. The sky’s color is therefore a useful reminder that the atmosphere does not simply act like a blue screen. It redirects the colors already present in sunlight, and the viewing angle and air between you and the Sun shape what you see.
